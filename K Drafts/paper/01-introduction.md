# 1. Introduction

Suppose a trader buys 100 shares in a batch auction. The execution report gives a price and quantity, and the trader can compare the price with their limit. It tells them little about the choice between that price and the next tick. A bid that did not trade may have broken the tie. A public list of executions cannot account for that bid. Publishing all the orders would permit anyone to repeat the calculation, at the cost of disclosing trading interest that never reached the tape.

Frequent batch auctions (FBAs) collect orders over short intervals and execute eligible interest at a uniform price. Budish, Cramton and Shim proposed batching as a response to the advantage of reaching a continuous market a few microseconds ahead of another trader ([6](references.md#ref-6), [7](references.md#ref-7)). Aquilina et al. estimate that latency races accounted for roughly one fifth of trading volume in their sample of FTSE 100 stocks ([1](references.md#ref-1)). Batching changes how the venue processes simultaneous demand. It leaves a separate design choice about what the venue discloses. Budish et al. specify what the exchange announces after each auction ([6](references.md#ref-6)). Zhang and Ibikunle study the sub-second periodic auctions operated by Cboe Europe ([32](references.md#ref-32)). During price formation, Cboe publishes indicative prices and executable sizes when there is a match, subject to its price controls. The underlying book stays private ([11](references.md#ref-11)).

Our FinteQC paper proposed using zero-knowledge proofs to verify the clearing result of a frequent batch auction ([14](references.md#ref-14)). Here we specify the price rule and connect its proof to the recorded order book. We consider an exchange that receives orders privately and wants traders and outside observers to check the final clearing price. Our starting point is the auctioneer's ordinary calculation. The exchange first finds all ticks at which the largest ordinary volume would trade. It then compares the imbalances at those ticks. Even where the checks span a wide interval, the verifier needs only its endpoints, the selected tick and a few volumes. We express the checks as polynomial identities on the price grid and prove them against commitments to the book.

There are two distinct claims to verify. One concerns the price of a given book. The other concerns the choice of book supplied to the price calculation. A proof of the correct price on a book of the exchange's choosing does little for a trader whose bid was left out. We therefore open each batch with four empty committed columns and let a trader check the change made for their order. A separate argument at close restricts subsequent changes to additions. The trader can identify their contribution in the same history used to verify the price.

## Contributions

Our main contribution is a protocol for publicly verifying the clearing tick of a frequent batch auction without publishing the individual orders. [Section 5](05-security.md) states the integrity guarantee, the permitted disclosures and the remaining question about privacy of the full transcript. We express the exchange's price and rationing rule as constraints on a committed price grid. We also connect the final grid to an initially empty book through a proof of valid updates. Together these components let the public check the price and let a trader check that an acknowledged contribution appears in the history used to calculate it.

We built two implementations and a calibrated synthetic book generator to evaluate the protocol. Our Rust implementation realizes the committed-book protocol, including both proofs and trader receipts. Our Noir implementation independently expresses the crossing and no-cross relations on a finished book. This gives us a second computation of the same auction outcome. Our generator resamples Nasdaq order flow to produce books with price ties; published Cboe outcomes supply observed prices and sizes from an operating periodic auction.

## Related work

Prior auction protocols use cryptography to establish a result on hidden bids. Parkes et al. give a publicly verifiable sealed-bid auction ([26](references.md#ref-26)). Thorpe and Parkes show how an exchange can prove properties such as the spread and market depth while withholding individual orders ([30](references.md#ref-30)). Galal and Youssef use aggregated Bulletproofs to verify the ordering of committed values in a periodic auction ([17](references.md#ref-17)). FairTraDEX uses a commit–reveal mechanism for a decentralized frequent batch auction ([22](references.md#ref-22)). Secure multiparty computation can distribute trust in the auctioneer, as in the Danish sugar-beet auction and work on dark pools ([5](references.md#ref-5), [9](references.md#ref-9)). We retain an exchange that knows the orders. Our question is whether another party can check the result while the losing orders stay off the public record.

Our proof uses the price tick as its row index. This permits one running sum for demand and another for supply, regardless of how many orders contributed to an individual tick. Zeeperio provides a precedent for combining individual and public verification in an election ([21](references.md#ref-21)). In the auction, the object of individual verification is an addition to a volume column. The public outcome depends on the shape of demand and supply across ticks, including prices at which no trade ultimately occurs.
