\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\textbf{Proof latency.} On the benchmark laptop, the exchange computes the clearing receipt in 0.07 ms. It can announce the outcome at the close and generate its proofs while collecting the next batch. Traders can observe the reported price immediately. Public verification becomes possible once both proofs arrive. A delayed or missing proof leaves a publicly observable, unverified receipt. To sustain this schedule, the prover must keep pace with incoming batches on average.

\textbf{Throughput.} Each asset has its own book, so the exchange can assign separate proving resources to different assets. Within one asset, update validity becomes more expensive as the order log grows (Table 4). Using one row per order could increase the number of orders represented in a fixed domain fourfold (Section 4.3). The resulting proving time has yet to be measured.

\textbf{Public disclosure.} Before the Closing Cross, Nasdaq disseminates paired volume, imbalance size and side, and indicative prices {[}36, 37{]}. Cboe's periodic-auction feed reports an indicative price and size {[}15{]}. Our final receipt also publishes the plateau \([c,d]\), long-side volume \(P\) at the clearing tick, and eligible IO volume \(J\). Traders use \(P\) and \(J\) to compute fill ratios. The verifier uses the plateau endpoints to construct \(Mask_P\) and \(Mask_C\). Keeping the plateau private would require commitments to these selectors and constraints on their shape.

\textbf{Exchange discretion.} The exchange sees the order flow. It can refuse an order before issuing a receipt and can submit orders of its own. After acknowledging an order, it must clear a book consistent with the verified log. The protocol specifies pro rata fills before rounding. The venue must define how it distributes whole units. The reference string also requires an honest contribution to the setup ceremony.

\textbf{On-chain verification.} Our verifier runs off chain. A contract in the style of Zeeperio {[}31{]} would check the pairings and compute \(W\) from the logged steps. The latter operation grows linearly with the log. Our experiments cover the implementation choices in Section 5.1 on one laptop. They do not establish a gas cost.

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

We adapt a closing-cross rule to a private order book and encode its price and rationing decisions in 33 constraint groups. Traders can check their own recorded orders, and anyone can verify the clearing receipt and the non-negative updates to the book. In the 1,000-tick, 1,000-order benchmark, the two proofs occupy 7.8 KB and take 0.73 s to generate on eight laptop threads. The reported batch check takes 0.12 s. Across four days of replayed AAPL trading, 3.6\% of crossing one-second batches required a tie-break after volume maximization. Hiding orders from the exchange and proving integer fill rounding remain open.
